    What the workbooks do, step by step

    1. Raw counts → groups. Each morphotype column of Raw counts is copied to Sorted counts and summed into one group. Non-GSSC groups: AQ, PALM, ZINGI, Other FI, GRASS/MONO/CONI ND (NDG) and OTH (NDO). GSSC groups: BO and early-diverging, POOID-D, POOID-ND, PACMAD GEN, PANI, CHLOR and OTHG.
    2. Total count. Total = Σ non-GSSC morphotypes + GSCtot, where GSCtot is the total number of GSSC tallied in the Meltmount count (GSSC sheet column DY, copied to the non-GSSC sheet column GB).
    3. GSSC split. The identified GSSC (oil count plus any identified in the Meltmount, column DX) give the proportions, which are scaled to the Meltmount total:
    GSSC_k = g_k / Σg × GSCtot (GSSC sheet, Collapsed counts M:S)
    1. Percentages. Every group is divided by the total count: % = count × 100 / Total (Collapsed counts + GSSC, T:AF). These are the AQ … NDO columns of the Sample table.
    2. FI-t. Using the Sample table's own formula =SUM(J:L)*100/SUM(J:T):
    FI = PALM + ZINGI + Other FI FI-t = 100 × FI / (FI + BO + POOID-D + POOID-ND + PANI + CHLOR + PACMAD GEN) = 100 × FI / (FI + GSCtot × (1 − OTHG/Σg)) OTHG is left out

    The page recomputes all of this from the raw counts, whether they come from the built-in workbooks, workbooks you drop in, or the Data input sheet. For the built-in data every Sample table value matches the workbook to within floating-point rounding (largest difference shown on the Results tab).

    Bootstrap

    The workbook has a column for the FI-t 95% interval but no values or formulas behind it, so the interval is computed here. Each replicate redraws the counts with replacement and then runs the identical arithmetic above; the interval is the 2.5th to 97.5th percentile of the replicates (or the level you choose). Two resampling schemes are offered:

    • Both counts (two-stage), the default. (1) Redraw the Meltmount assemblage: Total phytoliths from the multinomial with the observed group proportions (AQ, PALM, ZINGI, Other FI, GSSC, NDG, NDO). This gives a new FI count and a new GSCtot. (2) Independently redraw the Σg identified GSSC across the seven GSSC classes. Then scale, take percentages and FI-t as in the workbook. This propagates both counting steps into every column, including the grass-community shares.
    • Diagnostic count only. The classic approach: treat FI + diagnostic GSSC as a single count of n phytoliths (rounded) and redraw it with the observed proportions. It leaves out the uncertainty from the GSSC identification count. For these samples it gives almost the same FI-t intervals as the two-stage scheme, because OTHG is a small share of the GSSC, but only FI-t gets an interval in this mode.

    Random numbers come from a seeded generator, and each sample's stream is keyed by the seed and its name, so the same seed gives the same intervals every time and adding a sample does not change the others.

    Count size

    Strömberg (2009) examined how count size affects phytolith assemblage results and how many diagnostic phytoliths are needed for a stable FI-t; this page marks samples below 200 diagnostic phytoliths with open dots, and grass-community estimates based on fewer than 20 diagnostic GSSC. The interval-width plot on tab 4 shows directly what extra counting would buy.

    Projects and photos

    Each project holds its own samples, counts, morphotype list and microscope photos. The menu at the top right shows one project, or every project together; in the combined view each sample is labelled with its project, and morphotypes are matched across projects by code and group. Counts are always edited one project at a time.

    In Chrome or Edge you can link a folder on your computer (Projects → Link a projects folder). Each project then lives in its own subfolder as project.json plus a photos folder; photos you copy into that folder in Finder appear after Refresh on the Photos tab. Chrome asks you to reconnect the folder once per session. In other browsers projects and photos are kept in the browser's own storage on this computer. A photo's date and time come from the camera metadata inside the file (EXIF DateTimeOriginal, or the TIFF DateTime tag); when there is none, the file's modified date is shown and labelled as such. TIFF files are decoded in the page.
