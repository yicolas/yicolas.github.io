Five Trips with Bob Sharp

Caltech Ge 136 and Ge 124a field excursions, 1994–1995: central Mojave and Landers; Owens Valley, northern Death Valley and the Racetrack; Baja California; Ventura and the Transverse Ranges; and a Cub Scout coast run. Transcribed and annotated from the original handwritten notebook, September 2026.

About this document

This is a transcription and annotation of a single handwritten field notebook kept on Caltech geology excursions between February 1994 and March 1995. Bob Sharp is the recurring figure in it: he is named as the leader of the first trip ("Bob Sharp, et al."), he narrates most of the stops on the others, and the notes repeatedly record his asides, arguments, and habits of attention. The notebook is not Sharp's own hand — it is a student or assistant's record of what Sharp and the other speakers said at each stop, written on the spot, with a GPS receiver in one hand.

That last detail shapes everything. The notebook was kept in the first years of civilian handheld GPS, and the writer logged latitude and longitude at nearly every stop, often with a note about how badly Selective Availability was degrading the fix ("No GPS scrambling," "Almost no GPS scrambling," "Big dither," "center point of ½ hr dithering average"). Selective Availability was the deliberate error the US military added to the civilian signal; it was not switched off until May 2000. So these coordinates are good to perhaps 50–100 m, sometimes worse, and the notebook itself tells you which ones to trust. The longitudes are all west and the latitudes all north unless the trip is in Baja, and they are written in degrees and decimal minutes.

How this write-up is organised

Each trip gets its own chapter. Within a chapter the stops appear in the order and with the numbering the notebook uses, each headed by its stop number and a short name, followed by a grey line giving the coordinates, time, and elevation exactly as recorded. The body of each entry is what the notebook says, unpacked from field shorthand into sentences. Where a name, term, or argument is compressed to the point of obscurity, a bracketed comment supplies the geological context that the note assumes; these additions are mine, not the notebook's.

Uncertain readings are marked with a bracketed question mark. The handwriting is fast and the notebook was rained on during the March 1995 trip, so a fair number of proper names are genuinely ambiguous — a few of these are flagged individually in the text and gathered again at the end. Coordinates are reproduced as written even where they are obviously mistyped in the original; where a digit appears to be an error, this is noted rather than silently corrected.

A word on what these notes are good for. They are not a published road log and were never meant to be one. What they preserve is better than a road log: the arguments as they were actually made in the field in 1994, several of which have since been settled, revised, or quietly dropped. The Blackhawk landslide lubrication debate, the Palmdale Bulge, the age of the Sierra Nevada uplift, whether the Willow Spring fault is active, and how many events built the Lone Pine scarp all appear here as live questions with named people on either side.

Trip 1 · Ge 136: Central Mojave Desert and the Landers rupture

25–27 February 1994. Bob Sharp et al. Pasadena to Cajon Pass, out along the Mojave River to Victorville and Sidewinder Valley, south to the Blackhawk landslide, then east onto the 1992 Landers rupture for two days of fresh fault scarps, then Joshua Tree, the Dale basin fans, and a camp at Amboy Crater, returning via the Pisgah field and Barstow.

The organising subject of this trip is the Eastern California Shear Zone: the belt of northwest-striking right-lateral faults that carries roughly a quarter of Pacific–North America motion through the Mojave and hands it north to Owens Valley and Death Valley. The trip ran nineteen months after the 28 June 1992 Landers earthquake (M7.3), and the second day is essentially a walk along its surface rupture while it was still crisp. Two of the photocopied handouts in the back of the notebook are figures from Hauksson and others' paper on the Landers sequence, one of them the now-standard map of the shear zone; those were plainly the reading for this trip.

Day 1 — Friday 25 February 1994: Cajon Pass to Landers

Stop 1 · The San Andreas fault at Cajon Pass

34° 16.037′ N, 117° 27.435′ W · 9:45 am · Exit I-15 at Kenwood Avenue, the "Blue Cut"

The trip begins on the master fault. Lone Pine Canyon, running southeast from the pass, is the San Andreas itself, and Lost Lake — a sag pond in the fault trough — lies just to the north of the stop. The notebook records seven terraces on Cajon Creek, of which five are displaced by the fault: a terrace flight of that kind is the standard way to get a slip history, because each successively older surface has accumulated more offset.

In Cajon Creek the fault cuts Pelona Schist, and the most recent break is at the north end of the exposure. The zone of displacement is narrow, about three feet wide. The key statement is the negative one: no displacement here in 225 years, which implies roughly twenty feet of locked, accumulated slip waiting to be released. Neither the 1857 Fort Tejon rupture nor the 1812 event propagated into this reach — 1857 died out to the northwest and 1812 broke a segment further southeast — so this stretch of the San Andreas is a gap with more than two centuries of strain on it.

Stop 2 · Overlook of the Cajon basin

34° 20.971′ N, 117° 26.313′ W · 10:36 am · elev. 4,330 ft · Exit I-15 at Oak Hill Road, right on Mariposa Road toward the Summit Inn

A short walk out onto a ridge scarp for the view. The Mormon Rocks are visible from here — steeply tilted Miocene Cajon Formation sandstone and conglomerate standing against the fault, and the note records the local history that San Bernardino was a Mormon town.

The geological argument at this stop is a provenance one, and it is the first of several on the trip where a rock type is used to reconstruct vanished topography. Clasts of Pelona Schist in the basin fill had to come from across the fault, which means the alluvial fan surface that delivered them must once have extended over to the San Gabriel Mountains. In other words, the fan was built across a drainage that the San Andreas has since sliced and translated, and the modern configuration — in which no Pelona source lies upstream — is a product of that offset. The note adds that the fan drowned larger stream valleys as it aggraded.

Stop 3 · The Mojave River at Stoddard Wells: a superimposed stream

34° 32.087′ N, 117° 17.221′ W · 11:37 am · Exit I-15 at Stoddard Wells Road east of Victorville, turn right (south), through the "not a through street" gate, to the middle of the old bridge

Here the Mojave River comes to the surface and there is always water flowing — a rare thing on a river that is dry over most of its length, and a consequence of bedrock forcing the water table up. A dolomite plant stands nearby, and downstream (north) of the bridge is a cement plant whose dust, the notebook observes with a question mark, visibly lightens the rock.

The landform lesson is the incised canyon where the Mojave River cuts straight through a granite ridge rather than going around it: a superimposed stream. The river established its course on a cover of softer material, kept its grade as it eroded down, and was let down onto a buried bedrock high that it then simply sawed through. The note counts about five major faults between here and Lake Manix downstream and asks whether the Afton Canyon level can be identified — Afton Canyon being the gorge the river cut when pluvial Lake Manix overflowed, so the question is whether a datum from that event can be traced this far upstream.

Sharp then digresses to William Morris Davis, the Harvard geomorphologist who spent his last years at Caltech, whom the notebook calls the "Peneplain Perpetrator." The gate at Caltech is named for him — the note credits the Seismo Lab with the naming. The pediment surface to the north of the stop is the landform Davis's cycle of erosion was invoked to explain, and Sharp's generation spent much of its career dismantling that explanation.

Stop 4 · Lunch in Sidewinder Valley

34° 37.454′ N, 117° 12.430′ W · 12:30 pm

Lunch among the Sidewinder Volcanic Series, Triassic volcanic rocks. The conical mountain to the south is Bell Mountain, and the subject is a hillslope one: the mountain's concave surface is eroded into a spectrum of fragment sizes, fine to coarse, because the Sidewinder volcanics disintegrate into different sizes depending on their texture. Large boulders make steeper slopes. This is a Sharp theme — that the grain size a rock weathers into controls the angle of the slope it builds, so that lithology is legible in profile shape from a distance.

East of the lunch stop are carbonate quarries: roof pendant marbles, good for cement, hanging in the Mesozoic granitic rocks.

Leaving, the party skirted the Helendale fault — one of the northwest-striking right-lateral strands of the Eastern California Shear Zone — and took the Lucerne Valley cutoff at 34° 41.355′ / 117° 5.782′. A marginal note attributes work in this area to Paul Stone's [?] thesis.

Stop 5 · The Blackhawk landslide

34° 25.271′ N, 116° 47.264′ W · 2:35 pm · quarry in the landslide deposit. Second quarry at 34° 25.348′ N, 116° 47.178′ W, 4:00 pm, 3,184 ft

The longest and most argued stop of the day, at one of the most-studied long-runout landslides on Earth. The numbers recorded are the famous ones: about 30,000 feet of transport from the San Bernardino Mountains for 4,000 feet of vertical drop, at an estimated 120–170 miles per hour, moving on the order of 10¹⁰ cubic feet of rock.

The mechanism recorded in the notes is the stratigraphic one: Furnace Limestone sits on top of the Old Woman Sandstone; the sandstone erodes out from under, the limestone collapses onto it, and the resulting mass runs out far beyond what simple friction allows. That excess runout is the problem. The notebook records the two competing lubrication hypotheses as live: compressed air (Ronald Shreve's classic proposal that the slide rode on a trapped air cushion, indicated with a question mark here) versus Jay Melosh's acoustic fluidization, which the note describes simply as "non-air lubrication." Melosh's idea, that vibrational energy within the moving mass momentarily relieves overburden stress and lets it flow, was then relatively new and remains the leading alternative.

The field evidence discussed: transverse and lateral ridges visible on air photos, the classic surface morphology of a long-runout slide; and "domino boulders," clasts fractured in place at the resting stage, which record that the mass was being held apart during transport and only came together when it stopped. Martin Stout found a carbonate fossil playa-lake surface associated with the deposit, radiocarbon dated at about 18,000 years.

The party walked up onto the slide. From on top you can see the hills the slide over-topped as it came down to the southeast — direct evidence of how much momentum it carried. To the north is a good pediment surface at the base of the hills, cut on granite, with a sharp break in slope at the base of the granites that the note carefully records as being independent of the basal units and the Old Woman Sandstone — that is, the piedmont angle is not controlled by a lithologic contact, which is the observation that makes pediments interesting and hard to explain.

Stop 6 · The Landers fault scarp at the split hills

34° 27.964′ N, 116° 30.731′ W · via Bessemer Mine Road north toward Soggy Lake, turning right at 34° 27.165′ / 116° 40.790′

First contact with the 1992 rupture. The locality is Judy Zachariasen's "split hills," and the note commends her geologic map. Zachariasen, then working with Kerry Sieh at Caltech, mapped the Landers surface rupture in detail; the split hills are where the rupture passed through and separated a topographic feature, giving a piercing point for measuring offset. The party camped nearby.

Day 2 — Saturday 26 February 1994: along the rupture to Joshua Tree and Amboy

Stop 7 · Caliche in the roadcut, Bessemer Mine Road

34° 27.441′ N, 116° 38.017′ W

A soils stop, exposed conveniently in the roadway. The distinction drawn is the old pedological one between a pedocal — a desert soil with a caliche (calcium carbonate) horizon, formed where evaporation exceeds leaching so carbonate accumulates at depth — and a pedalfer, the humid-climate soil in which iron and aluminium remain while carbonate is stripped out entirely, grading to laterite in the tropics. Caliche matters on this trip because a carbonate horizon's thickness and stage of development is one of the few ways to age a desert alluvial surface, which is exactly what the fault-offset dating on the next stops depends on.

Stop 8 · The Emerson/Landers fault scarp at Stanford Hill

34° 32.359′ N, 116° 33.250′ W · 9:38 am

The Emerson fault carried a large share of the 1992 Landers slip at its northern end. Here the notebook records a fault scarp about one metre high, together with neatly offset stream channels and ground cracks — in other words, the full textbook assemblage of strike-slip rupture morphology, nineteen months old and not yet softened. A photocopied block diagram in the back of the notebook labels exactly these features (scarp, bench, offset drainage channel, sag pond, shutter ridge, linear valley), and it is hard not to conclude it was handed out at about this point.

Stop 9 · Road offset by the fault

34° 32.811′ N, 116° 33.654′ W · 10:55 am · also the junction up to Stanford Hills

The single most legible measurement of the trip: the painted centreline of the road is offset by about 3–4 metres. The important qualifier is the second sentence — the displacement is distributed through a zone, not taken up on one clean break. That distinction, between the total offset across a zone and the slip on any one strand, is what makes measuring surface rupture harder than it looks, and it is why the road centreline is such a useful marker: it was a straight line across the whole zone before the earthquake.

Stop 10 · Lunch, granite boulder field west of Highway 247

34° 20.258′ N, 116° 29.647′ W · 11:56 am

A boulder field in granite — the same weathering theme as Bell Mountain, here in the corestone-and-grus form that granitic rocks take in the desert. From here the route continues to Reche Road and into Landers.

Stop 11 · Offset telephone poles on Reche Road, Landers

34° 15.941′ N, 116° 26.413′ W · 12:59 pm · just onto Reche Road

The best cultural piercing line on the trip. Where the Landers fault crosses Acoma Trail, the telephone poles are offset: about 2 m right-lateral on the east–west line of poles, about 1 m on the north–south poles. The difference between those two numbers is the point of the stop. A line of poles records only the component of displacement perpendicular to itself, so an east–west line across a northwest-striking right-lateral fault registers most of the slip, while a north–south line registers much less. Two lines at different orientations crossing the same fault therefore let you solve for the full slip vector rather than just one component.

The party then drove north on Acoma Trail to a second locality, and on toward Joshua Tree via Pipes Canyon.

Stop 12 · New Dixie Mine Road and Dusty Mile Road

34° 16.504′ N, 116° 26.921′ W · about one mile north · Dawn's Galaxy Motel

A negative result, honestly recorded: "No good view of fault here, or any more." The rupture has passed out of ground where it is easily seen. The party turned south on Pipes Canyon Road. An intermediate point on the road was logged for a participant named Rob at 34° 11.974′ / 116° 29.253′, 1:30 pm, without being called a stop.

Stops 13 and 14 · Botany, and a petrol station

Stop 13: left on Pioneertown Road, 34° 9.858′ N, 116° 31.209′ W, 1:36 pm · Stop 14: Texaco station, Yucca Valley, 34° 7.199′ N, 116° 26.197′ W

Two stops for Rob's botanical seed collecting — the notebook calls the second of them the "2nd botany stop" — and then fuel in Yucca Valley. Worth keeping in the record because they show the trip was not purely geological; a botanist was along and the itinerary bent to accommodate him.

Stop 15 · Joshua Tree National Monument: granite and Joshua trees

34° 0.706′ N, 116° 09.697′ W · 4,209 ft · 2:48 pm · a roadside stop, chosen for weathering displays

Granitic grus and boulders. The host rock is the White Tank Quartz Monzonite, a Late Cretaceous intrusion, cut by aplite dikes. East of the stop a roof pendant of dark Pinto Gneiss sits structurally on top of the White Tank monzonite — a pendant being a block of the older country rock left hanging in the intrusion. The notebook adds a sharp field caution: the boulders around the parking area are not the quartz monzonite, so do not take your hand-specimen impression of the pluton from them. Mio-Pliocene volcanic rocks are also present.

The natural history is recorded with evident enjoyment. Joshua trees are lilies — members of the lily family, not trees at all in any useful sense. They were once mined (harvested) for paper pulp, and they are not fire adapted, which is why fire is so destructive in this landscape. Joshua Tree National Monument began as a ranch whose owner protected the trees.

Stop 16 · Joshua Tree visitor centre

34° 7.713′ N, 116° 2.250′ W · 2,074 ft · 3:30 pm

A logistics stop, logged for completeness.

Stop 17 · Bajada and dissected fans above the Dale basin

34° 9.931′ N, 115° 52.365′ W · described as a "random stop to see an alluvial fan"

One of the better geomorphology stops of the trip, and a genuinely unresolved one. The setting is a bajada — coalesced alluvial fans merging laterally into a continuous apron along the mountain front. The observation is that the fans to the north are being dissected, with young and old surfaces clearly distinct from each other, and the question Sharp puts is simply: why dissection? The notebook answers "unknowing" — nobody at the stop had a satisfying explanation.

The relevance of the setting is that all these fans drain into the Dale basin, a closed basin with no outlet, formerly a pluvial lake, lying to the east and southeast. A closed basin has a fixed base level, so fan incision cannot be explained by base-level fall in the ordinary way, which is what makes the dissection interesting. Climate-driven changes in sediment supply and runoff are the usual candidate.

Two leads are recorded for anyone wanting to pursue it: Ward Smith at the USGS in Menlo Park, and the fact that Menlo Park holds 1950s-era cores from many of these lake basins — the kind of archive that could date the lake phases against the fan surfaces. The party then took Amboy Road north over the Sheep Hole pass.

Stop 18 · Salt crusts

34° 27.363′ N, 115° 44.452′ W

Recorded in the notebook with unusual economy and unusual enthusiasm: "Salt crusts — neat!" This is Bristol Dry Lake or its margin, a playa in the same chain of closed basins; the salt crusts are evaporite precipitated from brine drawn to the surface.

Stop 19 · Camp at Amboy Crater

34° 33.408′ N, 115° 46.970′ W · 5:33 pm · turnoff from the old highway at 34° 33.621′ / 115° 46.612′

Camp for the night at the foot of the cinder cone.

Day 3 — Sunday 27 February 1994: Amboy Crater and the Pisgah field

The day opens with a general briefing on the Amboy–Pisgah volcanic field, recorded as a block of notes before the first numbered stop. The regional volcanism is placed as early-to-mid Miocene and late Cenozoic, with the young activity being Plio-Pleistocene alkali basalt. The scientific interest is that these basalts are a probe of the upper mantle: alkali basalts rise fast from depth and carry mantle xenoliths, so the field is studied less for its landforms than for what it brings up. Amboy Crater itself is about 200 feet high and 1,500 feet in diameter, built in six eruptive phases, and the note flags pointedly that the flows are not dated — whereas the Pisgah flows, nearby, are easier to date. The lava is highly vesicular and carries titanaugite and labradorite, a mineralogy consistent with the alkaline affinity.

Stop 20 · Walking round Amboy Crater

Access easiest on the west side · first spring locality 34° 32.605′ N, 115° 47.322′ W, 10:23 am, about 0.72 miles from the car · "Rob's anomalous metallic rock site" 34° 32.602′ N, 115° 47.254′ W, 618 ft, 10:36 am

Volcanic bombs are found on the north and east sides. A depressed basin south of the crater has a stream channel on its east side. The stratigraphic puzzle set at this stop is a relative-age one: on the east side of the crater, fine-grained ash is buried by more blocky material — so which is older? The note works it through, observing that the fine-grained material is rilled, which argues it has been exposed to erosion longer and is therefore the older, and asks whether the coarse blocky stuff is the younger unit despite the ambiguity of the contact.

Springs occur on the southeast side of the mountain, and the notebook identifies these as the water source for the surrounding area. A researcher recorded as Ferber [?] found tufa deposits at Summit Lake — crack-filling carbonate, the kind of deposit that records a former high water table or standing water.

Stop 21 · A small cinder cone west of the highway

34° 35.585′ N, 115° 55.452′ W · 11:25 am

Nice volcanic bombs. The instruction here is a petrological one: look for xenoliths in the bombs — an orange matrix with green crystals, and also rare granitic lithics. The green crystals in an orange (iddingsite-altered) matrix are olivine, and green olivine-rich nodules in an alkali basalt bomb are mantle peridotite xenoliths, brought up intact because the magma rose too fast to digest them. This is the payoff of the earlier remark that these basalts are a way to study the upper mantle. The granitic lithics, by contrast, are crustal material picked up on the way through.

Stop 22 · Somewhere near the Pisgah fault

34° 47.707′ N, 116° 30.532′ W · 1:33 pm · via I-40 to Hector Road, back on the National Trails Highway

Recorded honestly as a question: "Pisgah fault somewhere around here?" The Pisgah fault is another northwest-striking right-lateral strand of the shear zone, and part of the day's point is how hard these faults are to find on the ground between the fresh 1992 rupture and the well-expressed range-front strands.

Stop 23 · BLM Desert Information Center, Barstow

34° 53.399′ N, 117° 01.349′ W

The last logged point of the trip.

Interleaf · A loose page of earthquake damage notes

Between the Mojave trip and the Cub Scout outing sits a single page in pencil, in a looser hand, with no date, no coordinates, and no stop numbers. It reads, in full: "Get off on Tampa. Building fell on car — started battery — set fire to building. Apartment building damaged. Half a block east of Zelzah on Kenzie [?]."

Tampa Avenue, Zelzah Avenue and the Reseda area are in the northwestern San Fernando Valley, and the page is almost certainly a route note for looking at damage from the 17 January 1994 Northridge earthquake (M6.7), which struck five weeks before the Mojave trip and devastated exactly these streets. The sequence described — a building collapsing onto a parked car, the car's battery shorting and igniting, the fire spreading back into the building — is the kind of secondary-effect anecdote that gets retold on reconnaissance trips. Read that way, the page is a small reminder of what the 1994–95 field season felt like in southern California: Landers was nineteen months old, Northridge was five weeks old, and the trips in this notebook are being run by people who had just spent a year looking at fresh ruptures.

Trip 3 · Owens Valley, Northern Death Valley and the Racetrack

20–22 May 1994. Caltech to Palmdale and out the Antelope Valley, north up Highway 14 past Mojave and Red Rock Canyon to the Coso field and Owens Lake, camp at Diaz Lake; then a morning on the 1872 Lone Pine fault scarp, north to Independence and Big Pine, east over the Westgard/Death Valley road into Eureka Valley and northern Death Valley, past Ubehebe Crater to camp at the Racetrack; then the Racetrack, out through Hunter Mountain and Saline Valley, and home down Highway 395.

This is the richest trip in the notebook and the one with the most named speakers: Sharp narrates, but Liz Holt-Warner talks on the Coso volcanics, Adam Showman on glaciation, Dave Evans on the Sierra Nevada block, Sam Webb on crustal gravity, and a participant recorded only as Albert H. on the Independence dike swarm. The thread running through the three days is the northern continuation of the Eastern California Shear Zone — the Owens Valley and Furnace Creek–Northern Death Valley faults — set against the Sierran frontal fault and the volcanism that accompanies the extension.

Day 1 — Friday 20 May 1994: Palmdale to Owens Lake

Departed Caltech 8:15 am via the Angeles Crest.

Stop 1 · Lamont Odett Vista Point, Palmdale

34° 32.465′ N, 118° 7.375′ W · 9:18–9:51 am · "No GPS scrambling"

An overlook of the Palmdale Reservoir and the East Branch of the California Aqueduct, with a talk on aqueduct history. The geological point is that Palmdale Reservoir occupies a natural sag pond — a depression in the San Andreas rift zone, impounded because the fault zone here is a trough rather than a single break. Levi Noble's mapping is credited for the rift zone; Noble spent decades on this stretch of the San Andreas and his maps were still the working basis in 1994.

The Anaverde road cut runs through the fault zone, and the Anaverde Formation is gypsiferous — gypsum was mined from it. The alluvial fan here is about 4,000 feet thick.

The stop then turns to one of the great cautionary tales of 1970s geophysics: the Palmdale Bulge. Bob Castle, a USGS neotectonicist, reported from repeat line levelling that the ground around Palmdale had risen roughly 25 cm, which was taken seriously as a possible precursor to a great San Andreas earthquake and drove a large monitoring effort. Dave Jackson at UCLA argued it was an artefact of systematic error in the levelling. The notebook records the awkward coda — "had collapsed??" — the bulge having apparently subsided as mysteriously as it rose, which is what one would expect if it had never existed. It is included on the trip as an object lesson in believing a signal that lives entirely inside a measurement technique.

Stop 2 · The sunken freeway at Rosamond, and the Tropico Formation

34° 51.889′ N, 118° 10.171′ W · 10:10–10:30 am · exit at Rosamond / Edwards AFB, 34° 51.830′ / 118° 10.209′; parked at the Arco AM/PM · "No GPS scrambling"

Sharp opens with a puzzle that is not geological at all: why is three and a half miles of the Highway 14 freeway depressed about thirty feet below the surrounding plain? The answer is engineering — the fill was needed to elevate the crossing overpasses, and in Lancaster the freeway was raised instead so that the street grid could pass under. It is a characteristic Sharp move, training the group to ask why a landscape looks the way it does before assuming the cause is natural.

Then the real subject. After the alluvial fan, hills rise suddenly, and the Willow Spring fault runs east–west along their base. The hills are Miocene volcanic rocks, lake beds and mudstone of the Tropico Formation — named, the note records, after the Tropico Gold Mine. Sharp thought the Willow Spring fault might be active.

About thirty miles east, the same Tropico Formation hosts the borate deposits at Boron (Kramer) — the largest borax deposits in the world. The discovery story is recorded: around 1915 a water well was drilled and hit pure borate mineral, and the deposit was subsequently worked as an open pit. Colemanite and ulexite are named; the sodium borate is the valuable phase because it is easy to process, and there are on the order of a hundred industrial uses for boron. Large waste piles are visible. Soledad Mountain, nearby, is granitic rock with Tertiary volcanics.

A human aside worth keeping: it once took Sharp three days to drive from Ventura to Bishop, and Willow Springs was a stop on that old road.

Stop 3 · Gold leach piles and carbon black, Highway 14

35° 0.715′ N, 118° 9.699′ W · 10:44 am · on Highway 14 across from a gold leach pile

An industrial-geology stop. The leach pit works low-grade ore: crushed rock heaped and percolated with cyanide solution to dissolve the gold. Stop 6 · Red Rock Canyon: three provinces, and the rise of the Sierra Nevada

35° 21.685′ N, 117° 58.800′ W · lunch, 12:10 pm onward · "Almost no GPS scrambling"

The longest stop of the day and the intellectual centre of it. A speaker recorded as Frank gives the geology.

The physical setting first: welded tuff breccia caps the red rock cliff, and the cliff itself is badlands cut in fragmental volcanic debris. The notebook observes that the basin was very similar to Rainbow Basin, the well-known Miocene lake basin near Barstow.

The stratigraphy is the Dove Spring Formation (the notebook credits a Burbank paper), with about forty lava flows intercalated through it, its lower part derived from a volcanic source. The key argument is that changes in source region recorded by the clasts reflect tectonics, and the sequence Frank lays out is a chronology built from provenance:

  • 17–18 Ma — volcanic eruption.

  • 17–15 Ma — east–west extension.

  • 10 Ma — left-lateral motion begins on the Garlock fault, inferred from new clast abundances appearing in the section.

  • 9 Ma — Basin and Range east–west extension.

  • 8 Ma — granitic clasts of Sierran type appear, sourced from a range to the east, which is read as the start of Sierra Nevada uplift.

That last inference — dating the rise of the Sierra Nevada from the moment its granite starts showing up as gravel in a basin to the southeast — is the kind of argument that makes a sedimentary basin worth the trouble. Frank also notes that the El Paso fault is dip-slip, not strike-slip.

Red Rock Canyon sits at the junction of three provinces: Sierran, Mojavan, and Basin and Range. Three fossil-bearing intervals are preserved. The fauna and flora recorded are palm wood, acacia, and grazing animals, implying something like fifteen inches of annual rainfall — a far wetter Miocene than the present desert; sloth, deer, antelope, wolf, bear and lion are listed. Fine opals occur in the canyon to the east.

Two pieces of institutional history: the UC Berkeley palaeontological tradition of John Campbell Merriam and Andrew Lawson worked this section, and Red Rock Canyon held a small stage station and café called "Ricardo" on the stage-coach route — the name that ended up on the formation — and later served as a location for early Western films and the television series *Bonanza*.

Stop 7 · Basalt flows and a river-cut edge, Highway 14 near Little Lake

35° 51.349′ N, 117° 52.515′ W · 2:13–2:44 pm · Tehachapi [?] to the west, lava to the right

A stop about the interaction of lava and a river. The glacial-age Owens River cut through here several times, and the volcanic rocks are around 400,000 years old, in three lava flows dated approximately 400 ka, 100 ka and 40 ka.

The inversion of relief is spelled out: the old flows are on top and the younger flow, which filled the river channel, is lower. Stop 9 · Coso Junction: the Coso volcanic field

36° 2.723′ N, 117° 56.753′ W · rest stop at Coso Junction

A green patch to the west marks an old fault scarp, called the "Portuguese Bench" — vegetation lines are one of the standard ways to see a fault in the desert, because the fault plane acts as a groundwater barrier and forces water to the surface.

Liz Holt-Warner gives the Coso volcanics. The field consists of young rhyolite domes on north–south trending structures, underlain by Mesozoic plutons, and mapped by Charlie Bacon and Wendell Duffield (the notebook cites Duffield and Bacon 1981, USGS map I-1200). There is Holocene uplift and microseismicity.

The comparison everyone reaches for is Long Valley Caldera, and the answer given is no. The reasoning: there is no magma reservoir above 8 km depth, so Coso will probably not form a caldera, because a caldera requires a large shallow magma chamber to evacuate and collapse into. Instead the silicic magma chambers are kept active by basaltic magma pumped in from depth — a maintenance mechanism rather than a single large body. Hot springs are present, and a sketch in the notebook shows the domes enclosed by an arc of faults. Sugarloaf Mountain is the main dome. A remark that the next eruption is "~55,000 years away" reads as a recurrence-interval estimate rather than a forecast. The note that there may be no ash units because the magma comes up already degassed explains why an active silicic system has produced domes rather than plinian eruptions.

Two geothermal plants were then under construction, with 3 MW operational and 25 MW more being built. The party walked over the fence to see the volcanic field. Sharp also observed that the aqueduct's holding lakes serve to purify the water.

Stop 10 · The edge of Owens Lake

36° 17.667′ N, 118° 04.069′ W · 4:59 pm · at the edge of a cattle patch

A fan stop at the foot of the Sierra. The alluvial fans here are built by debris flows, and the discussion covers the tractive force of debris flows and the scaling relation that fan area is proportional to drainage basin area — one of the most robust empirical laws in geomorphology, because a bigger catchment delivers proportionally more sediment to build a bigger fan. Across Owens Lake is the Cerro Gordo silver mine.

Stop 13 · The Lone Pine fault scarp: how many earthquakes?

Park at 36° 36.291′ N, 118° 4.465′ W, 9:30 am, under the trees by the scarp · good view of the scarp at 36° 36.406′ N, 118° 4.510′ W · departed 10:38 am

The set-piece stop of the trip, and the one where the notes are densest. The scarp shows slight en-echelon cracks and, the note asserts, two or three displacements were required to produce it — that is, the scarp is cumulative, not the product of 1872 alone.

The geomorphic setting has to be established first. Big boulders in the scarp mark the former course of Lone Pine Creek; a small stream capture diverted the creek, so the party is standing on an abandoned fan surface. The boulders are debris-flow boulders, and three abandoned channels cross the fan surface. The debris flows are offset by the fault, and the offsets pre-date 1872. The fan surface was abandoned 10–20 kyr ago, which sets the maximum age of everything on it and makes the offsets datable in principle.

The argument then turns to how you read the number of events off a scarp. Two lines of evidence are recorded:

  • Scarp morphology. There was an argument about the rounding of the fault scarp as evidence for prior earthquake scarps. A fresh scarp is steep and angular; it degrades toward a diffusion-like profile over time. A scarp that shows a rounded upper element with a fresh face cut into its base records more than one event, and the note carefully lists the profile elements seen: fan surface, curve, steep face, debris. (Someone had dug at the base, which the writer notes drily.) The reference given is a guidebook by Gupta [?] and the AD Hirst [?] / GSA centennial guidebook.

  • Boulder weathering (Lubetkin's argument). Stanley Lubetkin worked on exactly this scarp. The logic: a boulder exhumed by faulting exposes progressively younger, less weathered surfaces downward. The top of a boulder carries a line of desert varnish and weathering; the base of the boulder records the first period of faulting. The notebook records a boulder with three (or four?) layers of weathering intensity — the top layer most weathered, an intermediate zone, and a clean base. Each band is a period of exposure, so each boundary is an event. Large potassium feldspar crystals are noted, along with drill holes in the rock, and a recollection that there used to be cracks in the ground here.

Then two offset measurements, made by walking north along the scarp:

  • Old channel, 36° 36.447′ N, 118° 4.573′ W, 10:12: offset about 30 m. Only one faulting event has occurred since this channel was incised at the scarp.

  • Offset debris flow of the metavolcanics, 36° 36.559′ N, 118° 4.601′ W, 10:21: a younger channel than the first, offset about 23 m — "a good example of the right-lateral offset fan."

A sketch accompanies these, showing the scarp trending across the page with debris flows crossing it and being stepped sideways, north to the right. The pairing of a 30 m offset on an older channel with a 23 m offset on a younger one is the heart of the stop: two markers of different ages recording different cumulative slip is how a slip rate and an event history get built.

Stop 14 · Moffat Ranch Road: the Sierran block and the Independence dike swarm

36° 41.035′ N, 118° 6.344′ W · 10:51 am · left on Moffat Ranch Road, park just short of the aqueduct · good view of the mountain front and the north end of the Alabama Hills

Three short talks at one viewpoint.

Dave Evans — evolution of the Sierra Nevada block. Pliocene stream gravels and volcanic rocks pre-date the uplift of the Sierran block. This is the same style of argument as at Red Rock Canyon, run the other way: deposits that lie across the range and are older than its relief bracket when the range went up.

Sam Webb — crustal gravity. Two-minute gravity maps of Owens Valley exist. The Tertiary basin fill is about 0.5 g/cm³ less dense than the surrounding rock, which is the density contrast that makes the valley show up as a gravity low and lets the fill thickness be inverted.

Albert H. — the Independence dike swarm. A remarkable body: 40 km wide, more than 250 km long, and offset 64 km by the Garlock fault — which makes it one of the best piercing markers for measuring Garlock slip anywhere. The dikes are dark-coloured diorite and granodiorite porphyry, chemically similar to each other, badly weathered or metamorphosed, dipping 60–90° and striking about N30W [recorded as "N130W"]. The composition is calc-alkaline. The age is 148 Ma, Late Jurassic, by U-Pb on zircon (Jim Chen). They were emplaced as one event with no cross-cutting relationships among them, and they sit in the middle of the regional plutonic history: Triassic and Jurassic plutons, then the dike emplacement, then the Cretaceous plutons.

Trip 5 · Ge 136: Ventura, the Transverse Ranges, and the Carrizo Plain

3–5 March 1995. The longest and most densely stopped trip in the notebook — thirty-nine numbered stops in three days — and the most local, beginning with a fault scarp a mile from campus. The route runs west out of Pasadena along the Foothill and Sierra Madre fault systems, across the Simi Hills and the Cretaceous–Palaeocene section, up the Santa Clara River to the St. Francis Dam site, north into the Ojai and Sespe country for the Ventura Basin section, over into the Cuyama badlands, onto the San Andreas fault in the Carrizo Plain, and home through Frazier Park, Neenach and Castaic.

The trip is organised around the Western Transverse Ranges — the east–west grain of mountains that cuts across the otherwise northwest-trending structure of California. It rained for much of the weekend, which the notes mention repeatedly, and a Saturday-morning presentation by José Hurtado on Transverse Range tectonics set up the whole thing. The photocopied handouts in the back include the plate-tectonic reconstructions and the block-rotation model that explain why this range exists at all: the Western Transverse Ranges are a crustal block that has rotated clockwise by roughly 90° since the Miocene, as the Pacific–North America boundary reorganised.

Participants named across the weekend: Bob [Sharp] and Gene, José Hurtado, Nathan Niemi, Steve Kirtner [?], Laura Wasylenki, Ashwin Vasavada, Chris Krok, Jean Hsieh, Adel, Stacy, Lisa Gunkell, and John.

Day 1 — Friday 3 March 1995: Pasadena to Steckel Park

Stop 1 · Lacy Park and the Raymond fault

34° 7.215′ N, 118° 7.471′ W · 8:15 am (departed 8:40)

The trip opens a few miles from campus with a 150-foot-high fault scarp — the Raymond Hill fault, on which the Huntington Hotel sits. The notebook records that backhoe trenching gives a last motion of 2–3 thousand years, which prompts the question Sharp evidently liked to put to students: what does "active" mean? Is the threshold 10 ky, or 790 ky (the Brunhes–Matuyama boundary, a common regulatory datum)? The Raymond fault becomes the Malibu fault westward, continuing to the Channel Islands, and terminates against the Foothill fault zone — a large structure whose eastern end nobody notices because a hotel is built on it.

The hydrology is the practical payoff. There is a closed depression here, a basin about five feet deep, and the note is careful: it is not a sag pond but an alluvial fan feature, from the canyon that drains down Lake Avenue. There was once a grist mill at the base of that stream, now an artesian site. The Raymond fault acts as a groundwater barrier: Pasadena alluvium is about 1,000 feet thick and wedges down to nothing at the fault, so the fault dams a groundwater basin. South Pasadena deepened its wells below the fault and drained the water table.

Sharp had a five-foot contour map of Pasadena, which is exactly the resolution needed to argue this. Stop 8 · Anorthosite gravels in the Las Posas Formation

34° 20.509′ N, 118° 54.181′ W · 12:35–12:54 pm · exposure on Highway 23 · "no dithering"

A provenance stop of the same kind as Cajon Pass on the Mojave trip. The Las Posas Formation is a Pleistocene marine conglomerate full of crystalline rock fragments, including anorthosite — a grey to purplish igneous rock consisting largely of calcic plagioclase. Anorthosite is rare and distinctive, and the obvious source is the San Gabriel Mountains anorthosite massif; but Lee Silver said it did not come out of the San Gabriels. Stop 9 · The burning shale: klinker in the Monterey Formation

34° 20.824′ N, 118° 54.804′ W · 12:59–1:25 pm · along the road by the klinker; parked about 100 m from the quarry entrance

One of the strangest stops in the notebook. The Miocene Monterey Formation shale here is rich in phosphate and in organic matter, and it has been burning underground — spontaneous combustion of the organic-rich shale, still burning in the nineteenth century and having burned, the note says, for thousands of years.

Lisa Gunkell gives the mineralogy: the klinker reached temperatures of about 1600 °C, which is a genuine pyrometamorphism, hot enough to melt the rock. The assemblage recorded is α- and β-cristobalite, cordierite, quartz, and obsidian — that is, silica glass and high-temperature silica polymorphs, formed by natural firing of the shale. The yellow rocks are phosphate-rich. The red and white varieties have the same iron content, which is the key observation: the colour difference is not compositional but redox, because carbon monoxide and hydrogen released during burning reduce the iron. Sharp attached a story about a Southern Pacific Railroad man to the stop.

Stop 10 · The St. Francis Dam disaster

34° 23.232′ N, 118° 54.972′ W · 1:30–1:58 pm · at the Fillmore city limit sign, Santa Clara River valley

Chris Krok gives the talk on the failure of the St. Francis Dam, the worst American civil engineering disaster of the twentieth century after the Johnstown flood. The figures recorded: about 4.5 million cubic metres of water released, and 450 people dead (the notebook's "7450" appears to be a slip of the pen).

The geology of the failure: the dam's west abutment was founded partly in Pelona Schist across a fault, and the notebook sketches the canyon cross-section with a fault on the west side and the schist beneath. The reservoir filled in 1926 and leaked along the fault gouge; by 1928 it was full and running muddy water; it collapsed at midnight on 12 March 1928.

The Caltech connection is recorded with some relish: Tom Clements, then a Caltech graduate student, was mapping the area in 1928, noted that a fault was present, and decided not to camp under the cottonwood trees the night of the collapse. The trees, and the ground they stood on, were swept away.

Across the valley, Oak Ridge is on the southern skyline, with skin slides visible to the southwest, and many landslides on the north side of Oak Ridge. The note draws a grim parallel to the death of some Princeton graduate students in a similar slide.

Stop 28 · Cavernous weathering and case hardening

34° 36.586′ N, 119° 22.004′ W · 1:58–2:05 pm · in the Coldwater Sandstone

Tafoni — the honeycomb hollows that develop in sandstone cliffs. The mechanism argued is case hardening from airborne debris, credited to Jim Concar [?] and Roosman [?], with boron distribution in the Valley of Fire cited as supporting evidence. The emphatic note is the negative one: not wind erosion. The surface crust forms by mineral precipitation at the rock face, and where the crust is breached the softer interior weathers out, producing the cavity. The wind removes the debris but does not do the cutting — a distinction Sharp's generation had to make repeatedly against the older aeolian-abrasion explanation.

Stop 37 · The Neenach Volcanics

Stop 39 · The Violin Breccia

Appendix A · The photocopied handouts

Tipped into the back of the notebook is a set of photocopies, folded and worn. They are not in trip order, but each can be matched to a stop. Together they are a useful index of what the trips expected participants to have read.

1 · "Ubehebe Craters" — a one-page summary with two figures

Plainly the handout for Stop 19 of the Death Valley trip. Its content, point by point: 13 to 16 volcanic maar craters of late Pleistocene or Holocene age, "maar" meaning lake, mostly under half a mile across and less than 500 feet deep, with Ubehebe itself 0.7–0.8 km in diameter and 235 m deep and Little Hebe 100 m across and 20 m deep. The origin is phreatic explosion — groundwater flashed to steam by rising magma — but magma and magmatic gases were involved too, since bombs are present and sideromelane shards indicate rapid quenching, which is diagnostic of a maar. The explosions blew through a fanglomerate sequence at least 500 m thick. Ejecta covers about 15 km², reaching about 50 m thick at the rim of the largest crater; the largest crater formed last, and its ejecta blankets all the pre-existing craters. The large crater is centred on a north-trending fault zone. Pinkish quartzite torn from the east wall during the eruptions occurs as cubical fragments nearby, and stream-rounded pebbles show sudden-heating fracture.

The figure is Moore (1967), an index map giving the probable sequence of eruptive events across the Ubehebe, Western and Southern crater clusters and Little Hebe.

2 · Base-surge deposits — a second page, with figures from Crowe and Fisher (1973)

The technical companion to the above, and the reason a planetary-science department cared. It distinguishes airfall deposits — thinly bedded continuous sheets, not cross-bedded, normally graded — from base surge deposits, produced by the ring-shaped basal cloud that sweeps outward from the base of an explosion. Base-surge deposits move radially outward, are cross-bedded, generally lenticular, poorly sorted, and lack grading; flow direction can be recovered from bedding (ripples, dune-like structures, sorting between lee and stoss sides) and from impact sags beneath ballistic clasts.

Two figures accompany it: a sequential diagram at 5, 10 and 40 seconds after a burst, showing the explosion column forming, the crater enlarging and overturning its rim flap, and the surge detaching and running outward; and a map of Ubehebe with measured bedding-sag trajectories and cross-bed current directions radiating from the crater. The closing line is the payoff: impact craters on Mars and the Moon may exhibit features resulting from base surge, which is what makes a maar in Death Valley a planetary analogue.

3 · Two figures from Hauksson and others on the Landers earthquake sequence

The reading for the February 1994 Mojave trip. One figure is the Eastern California Shear Zone map (after Dokka and Travis 1990), showing the shear zone as a band running from Indio through the Mojave into Owens Valley and Death Valley, with the Garlock and San Andreas faults for reference and the epicentres of the M6.1 Joshua Tree, M7.3 Landers and M6.2 Big Bear events. The second is the aftershock map, April–December 1992, showing the dense north-northwest streak of the Landers rupture, the Big Bear cluster striking off it at a high angle, and the Joshua Tree sequence to the south, with focal mechanisms for all three. Someone has written "Xai — 2/26/94" at the top, the date of the second day of that trip.

Appendix C · What recurs across the five trips

Reading the notebook straight through, a handful of preoccupations surface again and again, and they are worth naming because they are what a Sharp field trip was actually teaching.

Ask why the landscape looks like that — including when the answer is a bulldozer

The sunken freeway at Rosamond, the backwards freeway at Pyramid Lake, the depressed basin at Lacy Park that turns out to be a fan feature rather than a sag pond, the diverted Gower-Gulch-style drainages: over and over the group is handed a landform and made to work out whether it is tectonic, fluvial, or an artefact of engineering. Getting that wrong in either direction is the failure mode, and the notes show Sharp deliberately mixing the cases so that the answer can never be assumed.

Provenance as a clock

Pelona Schist clasts at Cajon Pass, Sierran granite appearing in the Dove Spring Formation at 8 Ma, anorthosite in the Las Posas Formation that Lee Silver says is not San Gabriel anorthosite, the Neenach–Pinnacles match: the same argument in four settings. What a basin is receiving tells you what was standing upstream at the time, and a change in what it receives dates a tectonic event.

How many earthquakes made this scarp?

The most technically sustained thread in the book, worked at Cajon Pass (225 years of locked slip), along the Landers rupture (a 3–4 m road offset distributed through a zone; poles at two orientations resolving the slip vector), and above all on the Lone Pine scarp, where scarp-profile rounding, three or four bands of boulder weathering, and two offset channels of different ages are brought together. The 1994 answer at Lone Pine — two or three events — was reached by triangulating morphology, weathering, and offset markers, with no cosmogenic dating available.

Faults as plumbing

The Raymond fault damming the Pasadena groundwater basin and being drained by South Pasadena's deeper wells; vegetation lines marking scarps west of Coso; springs on the southeast side of Amboy; the St. Francis Dam leaking along fault gouge. A fault is a hydrological structure before it is a seismic one, and in this landscape it is usually the water that makes it visible.

The value of the negative observation

"No good view of fault here, or any more." "The boulders around the parking area are not the quartz monzonite." "Not a fault scarp — river-cut edge." "Not a sag pond." "Not wind erosion." "Probably not form caldera — no magma chamber." "Lee Silver says it did not come out of the San Gabriels." The notebook is full of things carefully ruled out, and the habit of writing those down is most of what separates a field notebook from a travelogue.
